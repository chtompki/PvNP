    \textbf{(1)}
    \section*{DEFINITIONS}

    Before going into the proof we need to clarify what the complexity classes \textbf{P} and \textbf{NP} are.
    Note we could go into extensive details about \emph{deterministic Turing machines} and more importantly
    \emph{non-deterministic Turing machines} (more specifically Turing machines that are \emph{not} deterministic),
    but we need not add that amount of complexity for a paper that needs to be understood by grade schoolers.

    We mainly concern ourselves with the English verbal definitions here, as we need no more than that for our proof.

    \textbf{P} is the \emph{deterministic polynomial time algorithms}.

    \textbf{NP} is the \emph{non-deterministic polynomial time algorithms} or more importantly
    \emph{the \textbf{not} deterministic polynomial time algorithms}.


    \textbf{(2)}
    \section*{CLAIM AND PROOF}
    \begin{theorem}
        $\textbf{P} = \textbf{NP}$ and $\textbf{P} \neq \textbf{NP}$, simultaneously.
    \end{theorem}

    \begin{proof}
        We begin by writing out \textbf{P} and \textbf{NP} in the English as stipulated above with assuming they are
        equal. So we set:
        \begin{eqnarray*}
            \text{polynomial time algorithms} & = & \text{non-deterministic polynomial time algorithms} \\
            \text{(polynomial time algorithms)} & = & \text{non-(deterministic polynomial time algorithms)} \\
            \text{(polynomial time algorithms)} & = & \text{not-(deterministic polynomial time algorithms)} \\
            q & = & \text{not } q,
        \end{eqnarray*}
        where $q=\text{``polynomial time algorithms''}$. But $q = \text{not } q$ is simply the liar's paradox,
        otherwise known as \emph{The Great Antinomy} in the \emph{Principia Mathematica}. \textbf{QED}
    \end{proof}

    So the \textbf{P} versus \textbf{NP} problem is independent (i.e. is simultaneously \emph{true} and \emph{false}).
    A more complex combinatorial proof is that it reduces to the Continuum Hypothesis, an exercise left to the reader
    after the hit and needed set-theory correction (note: set-theory has strayed from the original definitions unfortunately)
    that $\aleph_0$ is the size of the \emph{arbitrarily large finite integer $n$}, as given by Cantor and Russell.
